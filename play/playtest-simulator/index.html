The Guinea Pigs

A party of four imaginary adventurers who walk into any dungeon you throw at them thousands of times, to help you identify the encounters and traps your real players won't walk away from.

Or, how trying to build a D&D adventure inspired by The Legend of Zelda led me down the rabbit hole of simulation playtesting.

~11 min readCode on GitHub ↗

The perils of homebrewing

I'm quite fond of building homebrew adventures for the D&D games I run, especially when they draw cues from my favourite media. Heck, the entirety of the world of my home campaign builds itself as it goes, loosely connected by one-shots, character-driven arcs and pop-culture references.

However, the risk of writing your own adventures from scratch is that playtesting isn't ... really a thing. At least, not for me. Most of my sessions are built on a fairly loosey-goosey understanding of D&D 5E's systems, held together by panicked improv and a sense of "Welp, if they die, they die." So I often find myself realizing mid-session that oops, the boss is going to absolutely crush the party if I run it purely as written. It's even worse when you realize no one opened what you thought was a plainly obvious Secret Door as you're packing your dice up.

(If any of my players read this, shush, no you didn't. Go to someone else's corner of the internet and I'll see you this weekend.)

With all of this preamble established, this story begins one fine October evening. I love the atmosphere of the dungeons in the Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess, and I was about halfway through my attempt to translate one of them, Arbiter's Grounds, into an adventure.

The adventure asked a group of adventurers to search a ruined desert fortress for proof that dragons and their fire goddess were there before an ancient empire that turned it into a prison. Inside, they must destroy the ghosts of four judges, each of whom frees a sacred flame they carry in a lamp. That opens the way to a cursed executioner's sword in the depths, and then to the reanimated skeleton of a dragon in an arena above.

I was putting my finishing touches on the final boss battle with said reanimated dragon skeleton, when it occurred to me that the gauntlet of enemies leading up to the fight might be softening up the party a bit too much. And so I did what anyone with a paid Claude subscription would do, and asked it for its opinion, using my party's character sheets as a reference:

Vikrant

You have to simulate a playthrough of Arbiter's Grounds. Choose three parties, of 4 PCs, 5 PCs and 6 PCs respectively in order to successfully complete the dungeon. Pick your characters and then tell me the results.

Claude

All three parties finished the dungeon, and only one character died.

  • The 4-person party finished with no deaths. Their paladin destroyed the cursed sword in two rounds, and they beat the dragon skeleton in three.
  • The 5-person party ran into nine patrols, when about six were expected, and the attrition wore them down. They still won, but the dragon's breath killed their bard outright.
  • The 6-person party also finished with no deaths, despite arriving a day late and on their last water.

Dice were rolled for travel, patrols and the decisive moments, and the rest was estimated from average damage. Treat these results as a stress test, not a full play-by-play.

What this says about your adventure: the cursed sword is too easy for a prepared party, while the dragon's breath decides the final fight. It can drop a level 4–5 spellcaster in one go, so the battle ends up either lethal or trivial.

I'd intentionally built this dungeon to be a battle of attrition, so seeing that these simulated adventurers had run roughshod through the dungeon made me suspicious.

Vikrant

How thorough was this simulation?

Claude

Less thorough than my write-up made it sound. Some of it was solid, but a lot of the combat was me deciding outcomes, not dice ... Treat the fight-by-fight story as an illustration, not data.

Wow, okay thanks Claude. It was clear we needed a more comprehensive approach.

97,500 dungeon crawls later, that tidy little story did not survive. But then again, neither did the adventurers. Parties of the same sizes, built from my players' characters, didn't all stroll out with one casualty between them. The four-player party finished about a third of the time, and the five- and six-player parties surprisingly did worse, partly because I'd overdone how difficult the adventure got when there were more players present than I'd planned for.

It also answered the question I'd started with. At Level 5, the dungeon wasn't what was stopping people: most parties reached the boss, which then ended somewhere between 40% and 47% of all runs. At Level 4, which is what I was initially intending to run the adventure at, the party finished about 2–3% of the time, and most of them never saw the boss at all.

As helpful as this little simulation engine seemed at first blush, it could only play through this one adventure. The rooms were abstracted into code, and some of the module's more ... specific rules had leaked into the combat engine itself. Great for a one-time use, but redundant the moment the story concluded.

The next question was the interesting one: what would it take to play any adventure?

Meet the Guinea Pigs

The most essential ingredient to a good adventure is a group of intrepid heroes to go on said adventure. I mean, I'm no expert, but I'm pretty sure that's what Campbell was going for.

A simulator built around my players' characters is great, but it would only answer questions about their table. Instead, I recruited the Guinea Pigs: four of the most ordinary, stereotypical adventurers I could think of. Standard ability scores, their class's default gear and spells, nothing clever. They're the control group.

If more party members are needed, the simulation would then bring in Grukk, the Half-Orc Barbarian and Lyra, the Half-Elf Bard.

Now these sound like a lovely group of people in isolation, but they also need ideas and opinions on how to approach an adventure. So on every run, each of the Guinea Pigs is given a new personality: three scores between 0 and 1 for caution, curiosity and boldness.

  • Caution is how carefully the party looks after itself. A cautious party rests sooner, and takes a breather before a room it believes holds a boss if it's running low. It also pulls out of a losing fight earlier, instead of fighting to the last.
  • Curiosity is how much it wants to know what's behind the next door. A curious party is more likely to take the side passage instead of heading straight for the goal, and more tempted to open things it probably shouldn't.
  • Boldness is its appetite for a fight it could walk away from. Each Guinea Pig's boldness is drawn as roughly the opposite of their caution, with a little wobble, so a cautious adventurer is rarely also a bold one.

When the party has to decide something together, the loudest voice speaks for it: whoever feels most strongly, in either direction, sets the party's score for that trait. If the rest of the party clearly leans the other way, that run's leader settles it, which happens in about one run in four.

Some choices belong to one person, though. When the party finds something it probably shouldn't open, it's the most curious member who decides, and the log names them.

None of these decide anything alone. At every choice the party also weighs what it can actually see: how hurt it is, how many spells and hit dice it has left, and what a room shows on the way in.

That's how one party stands in for every kind of table. Try it: these are the actual formulas from the code. The first three are the party's habits, read from its combined scores. The last is the kind of choice one person makes alone, so there the sliders are the most curious member's own. Boldness doesn't get a slider, because the adventure we're about to send them into never asks about it.

Each choice is scored, then gets a little random noise so near-ties can go either way. Every choice is logged with its reason. Shown at 1st level. The first three formulas belong to the engine and work the same in any adventure. In a party of four, caution never changes when it runs: it's always once the second member falls. Try five or six. The last is written by each adventure; these numbers come from the one we're about to visit.

A dungeon, written as data

With our adventurers recruited, we now need a quest for them to go on. Now sure, I could use one of my own for this part, but that feels a bit too self-indulgent. Besides, there's always the risk of spoilers.

(Seriously, if you play in my campaign and you're still reading, close this tab and think about your choices.)

Instead, I'm going to use Matt Colville's The Delian Tomb, which is the gold standard for first-time players. In this adventure, goblins have kidnapped Bess, the blacksmith's daughter, and the party must track them through the woods to a desecrated tomb to bring her home.

Now at the highest level, an adventure can be abstracted into a map with a series of rooms joined by doors. Each room can hold monsters, a trap, a skill check, loot, or specific terrain challenges. Here's a real room from the tomb, and the secret door out of it:

m.node('4', name='4 Hall of the Oathkeeper',
       encounter='bugbear, goblin shaman', importance='boss', prepare=True,
       terrain=dict(distance=20, cover=dict(monsters=2)),
       checks=[dict(kind='flag', flag='secret_door', chance=0.55)],
       loot=dict(potions=2, gold='2d20+10', flag='bess'))

m.edge('4', '5', gate=lambda run: run.flags.get('secret_door'),
       label='the secret door ("I give my word")')

Here's what this room looks like in the original module.

From The Delian Tomb by Matt Colville, published by MCDM.

And here's how the code above maps onto it:

  • encounter is the bugbear and the goblin shaman. importance='boss' and prepare=True tell the party this is a room worth resting before.
  • terrain starts the two sides 20 feet apart, with cover for the monsters.
  • loot is the bugbear's nest, 2d20 + 10 gold, and Bess.
  • checks is the inscription. The simulator can't actually say "I give my word", so chance=0.55 is my guess at how often a table works it out. The edge below it only lets the party through to the tomb if it does.

This is ... pretty much all of the code any given room needs. The whole adventure comes to 76 lines, written straight from the module. The simulator looks up any monster present in a room among the 334 listed in the 5E SRD, and reads anything else from plain text. The goblin shaman isn't one of the SRD's monsters, so the module writes it in, in the same English you'd find in a rulebook. I'll admit it's a simplification: the adventure gives the shaman a short list of cleric spells, and the module boils them down to one spell attack:

Hex Bolt. Ranged Spell Attack: +4 to hit, range 60 ft., one target.
Hit: 7 (2d6) necrotic damage.

Parse that and you have a monster that can fight. Every turn it weighs what it can do: multiattack, breath weapons, spells by slot, conditions, finishing off the wounded.

Anything an adventure invents that a field can't describe, like a lamp that reveals ghosts or the blade you have to pull out of a boss's skull to defeat, attaches to the engine through a hook. The engine stays clean: it knows 5E and nothing else.

One trip through the tomb

Here's what the simulator actually does, for one run out of 2,000, alongside what the module says is happening while it does it. Every roll below is from the log of run #4101645, which you can replay yourself.

The smithy

The module opens in the Green Dragon Inn, where Lars the blacksmith bursts through the door, smelling of sulfur: goblins have taken his daughter, Bess. There's nothing to roll in a tavern, so the simulator skips straight to Lars's smithy, where the goblins left their tracks.

Before the first die, each Guinea Pig draws a personality. Zirael is the most reckless of the four (caution 0.26, boldness 0.81), so Zirael speaks for the party's caution and boldness. Orik is the most curious (0.81), so Orik speaks for its curiosity. Zirael is also this run's leader, but the party never splits, so Zirael never has to step in. Then Orik kneels by the tracks: 15 + 3 = 18 against DC 10. At least two goblins dragged something north, into the Boar Wood.

The Boar Wood

The wood takes more than a day to cross, so the party makes camp in a clearing and keeps watch. The module rolls a d12 for the night, and the only thing that can happen is a curious boar wandering in on a 12. Tonight it doesn't. Sometimes, an adventure can involve the party simply walking from one room to the next, and the simulator walks it too.

The goblin patrol

Out of the trees, the stonework of an old tomb shows between the hills, and two goblins are patrolling the hilltop in front of it. In the module, a wounded goblin runs to warn the others, so how this fight goes decides what's waiting inside.

The party sneaks up as a group. Zirael rolls a natural 1, but the other three carry it, and they get a surprise round from 60 feet away. Orik's toll the dead and Clarence's javelin finish both goblins in two rounds, so nobody runs to warn the tomb.

The entrance

Two goblin guards stand at the tomb's broken stone doors, none the wiser. All four Guinea Pigs sneak up on them. Zirael's dagger and Orik's toll the dead drop one, and Pip's Sneak Attack drops the other before it can slip inside. One round.

At my table

When my own table reached these doors, one of my players dressed the whole party up as goblins with soot, mud and buttons for eyes, and walked them in without a fight. There's no field for that.

The offering room

Down the steps the air turns cold, and from somewhere deeper comes a rhythmic chanting: the goblins mean to sacrifice Bess to their god, Maglubiyet. At the bottom is a room of mouldy bedrolls and goblins, one more than the party: five.

Clarence's stealth fails, but the others carry it again. Clarence rolls a natural 1, Pip crits for 19, and Zirael's burning hands takes out two goblins, then two more. Two rounds, nobody hurt. On the wall, a carving shows knights holding back a tide of monsters, above an oath sworn "by honor of my word". The simulator can't read it, but it's the answer to a riddle two rooms on.

The trap

A twisting corridor leads deeper, with a pressure plate halfway down that swings a scythe from the ceiling. Pip misses it, 4 + 5 = 9 against DC 12, so Clarence, out in front, steps right on it, and dodges with a 21 against DC 15.

The module marks the next room as the place to rest before a boss. The party is at full health, so it walks straight in.

The Hall of the Oathkeeper

A stone knight towers over a makeshift altar, where the shaman chants over Bess, caged at the statue's feet. The bugbear who leads the band is waiting too, and the module warns that it's the first enemy here that fights cleverly rather than out of fear.

It goes badly, and fast. Four rounds later Clarence and Orik are the only ones still standing, without a scratch. We'll come back to how further down.

A decision

Bess is free. On the statue's base is the riddle: "If you are to keep this, you must first give it to me." A real table might puzzle over it for half an hour. The simulator rolls the 55% from the code above, and this time someone gives their word: a secret door grinds open. So the survivors choose whether to go through, and the log says why:

Inside a fight

A fight is where most of the code lives: attacks, saves, spell slots, concentration, conditions, reactions, legendary actions, death saves, morale. On each turn, a character scores every option against every target by expected damage, marks spell slots down a little so they aren't burned on goblins, and takes the best.

There's no battle map. Position is front rank, back rank and zones: how far apart the sides start, cover, high ground, chokepoints, darkness. It's a simplification, but it means a room can say "20 feet apart with cover" and that changes who gets to act. Here's that hall, round by round.

Then do it 2,000 times

So was that run typical? Not even remotely, and one run in isolation can't tell you that. One run is a story. Two thousand is data, and they take about two and a half seconds to finish. Here's what that looks like, with every square below representing one trip through Delian's Tomb.

About three runs in four end with nobody dead, and 57 end with the entire party dead. I've used "finished" generously here, though. It only means Bess got home safely, with no mind to the number of dead adventurers it took to do so. On the bright side, that means the run you just watched counts!

Then you change one thing and run it again. That's where the stock party earns its keep, because every difference comes from the thing you changed: fewer players, a higher level, a different ruling.

Can you trust it?

Of course, none of this matters if the numbers are wrong, and the new engine started life as a rewrite of the old one.

I built this with Claude, in conversation. Before any code changed, it read the original and sorted it into what would carry over and what wouldn't. About half was already general: the dice, the combat loop, the characters, the personality model. The rest was The Arbiter's Grounds' specific mechanics tangled through everything.

So we split the engine from the content, loaded the free rules' monsters in so that only bosses would need hand-written tactics, and then tried very different adventures to prove new ones don't need engine changes.

If the port can't reproduce the numbers you already trust, nothing built on top of it can be trusted either.

The new engine had to match the old simulator on every study. It matched within noise on all eight, and replayed 14,400 isolated fights and 600 player-versus-player fights exactly.

Did the fixes work?

They did! I rewrote parts of my Arbiter's Grounds using what the first report said: when wandering patrols can appear, how the two biggest bosses spend their turns, and some of the treasure. Then I had the module play the new text, with one setting that still plays the old rules. Old-rules mode has to match the original code exactly, and it did, on all 24,000 runs.

That makes before and after a fair comparison: same engine, same seeds, random personalities on both sides.

Every study got easier, but that doesn't say which change specifically caused it. So on one study, I had the simulator put each old rule back, one at a time. Only the patrol change was clearly bigger than noise, at about 3 points. The others were worth 1 to 1.6 points each, and one did nothing measurable.

And the numbers kept me honest about what I hadn't fixed. The dragon skeleton is still where most failed four- and five-player runs end, and I still haven't decided what happens when a party wipes late in the dungeon: death, or capture. That one ruling moves the four-player party's finish rate from about a third to about 70%.

What it's good at

What it's good at is the shape of a whole adventure:

Which fights actually killIn The Arbiter's Grounds, the dragon skeleton ended 40–47% of all runs at 5th level.
What content parties never seeOnly about a quarter of runs ever reach the Delian Order's tomb.
Which rulings swing the resultDeath or capture on a wipe moves one party's finish rate from about a third to about 70%.
What it can't do (yet)
  • No battle map. Zones and ranks stand in for squares, so flanking and clever positioning are rough at best.
  • Scripted players. Real tables are smarter and stranger. They'd look harder for that secret door, and do things no script would.
  • Thin outside combat. Talking, puzzles and mysteries come down to the odds of a skill check.
  • One stretch at a time. It won't carry a party through a whole season for you.

Not just dungeons

The same format stretches to the messier parts of a campaign. A module can include travel before the first room, wandering monsters, session breaks, and what a wipe actually means at your table: death, or capture.

The stock party can step aside, too. Give it your own characters, built from their PDF sheets, and each one plays to a personality score taken from their backstory.

And it doesn't have to start fresh. I use it on my own campaign's next session, with the party starting where the last one stopped: a setting can begin them at 60% health with half their spell slots gone, because that's how they'll really walk in.

The boss might still crush them, and nobody may open the door. Welp, if they die, they die. But at least now I know the odds before I sit down.
